### 1. Hosseini and Panwar, "Restless Video Bandits: Optimal SVC Streaming in a Multi-user Wireless Network," arXiv 1612.03985v2 (24 Aug 2017), 16 pp., NYU Tandon

- **Setting.** N users, M subchannels per slot (OFDMA), a finite Markov channel per user that sets how many SVC layers a subchannel carries, L layers per segment, a playback buffer per user. Each user's quality-adaptation (QA) policy is fixed by the content provider and given to the scheduler as a policy matrix (DBP with a prefetch threshold, CBP, BPP). The per-slot reward is a QoE of the playback rate, R = e^(−φ(R_p/R_max)^(−θ) + φ) with φ = 0.16, θ = 0.66, minus a rebuffering penalty.
- **Formulation.** Infinite-horizon discounted. QA-adaptive scheduling is a restless bandit, relaxed in Whittle's way: the discounted number of active arms is held to M/(1 − β) instead of M in every slot. The relaxed problem ("RB") is a linear program. For homogeneous users the optimal allocation can be taken identical across users (Thm 4.1, Cor 4.2; App. A, an averaging argument), so RB reduces to one user's LP. The jointly optimal QA-plus-scheduling problem is a multi-user semi-MDP (MUSMDP), used to measure the cost of keeping QA at the client.
- **Policies.** QAA (QA-aware): a primal–dual priority list of states from the LP's reduced costs, in the Bertsimas–Niño-Mora style. BEAS (QA-blind): a buffer-evolution heuristic. Baselines PF, best channel first, lowest buffer first. Heat maps of the priority (Figs. 7–8) show channel-driven priority above a critical load ρ* and buffer-driven priority below it.
- **The bound.** RB is plotted as the black line in the reward panels (Figs. 10a, 11a, 12a, 13a): 20 homogeneous users, 4–18 subchannels (load ρ = 1 to 5), and two 10-user classes. The text: "QAA performs very close to the optimum illustrated by the black line, especially for highly loaded networks". So **a relaxation value beside the policies, at a moderate size, is already in a wireless-video restless-bandit paper.** It is called the optimum, not an upper bound on causal schedulers, its tightness is not analysed, and N is fixed at 20.
- **Results.** Up to 2× network utilisation over PF (abstract); BEAS delivers "up to 30% more users" than PF at a fixed QoE; a 9-node ORBIT WiFi testbed (Fig. 14).
- **Not in the paper.** No finite horizon or terminal reward; layers decode or fail (digital), and the channel only sets how many fit; no SNR-graded quality; the channel state is observed, so no stale reports; no HARQ; no gap-versus-N study.
- **Verdict.** [DP] [UB]: HIGH. The report's open question ("if Hosseini and Panwar report the LP value, layered media already has a relaxation bound") is answered yes. What stays ours under [UB]: a finite-horizon bound with per-slot prices, used explicitly as an upper bound on every causal scheduler, with confidence intervals, a study of its gap against K, and decoded validation. Cite it as the closest wireless-video restless bandit.
- **Leads in its references.** [24] Fu and van der Schaar, arXiv 0903.0207 (the multi-user framework); [23] Xiao and van der Schaar, "Optimal foresighted multi-user wireless video," IEEE JSTSP 9(1), 2015.

### 2. Fu and van der Schaar, "Decomposition Principles and Online Learning in Cross-Layer Optimization for Delay-Sensitive Applications," arXiv 0812.1244 (submitted 31 Oct 2008), 30 pp., UCLA

- **Identity.** One user's data units, not multiple users. The multi-user paper the report cites, "A Systematic Framework for Dynamically Optimizing Multi-User Wireless Video Transmission" (IEEE JSAC 2010, arXiv 0903.0207), is a different paper and is still unread. The journal version of this one is, from memory, IEEE Trans. Signal Process. 2010; confirm before citing.
- **Setting.** One user transmits M data units (DUs), each with a deadline, a distortion impact and a size, over a time-varying channel; DUs are independent or linked by a dependency DAG.
- **Method.** The full-knowledge problem is a constrained nonlinear program. Its Lagrangian, with a resource price λ (lower layer) and "impact factors" μ for neighbouring DUs (application layer, for the FIFO coupling), splits it into one subproblem per DU plus two master problems updated by subgradient. Convexity gives a zero duality gap; DAG dependencies are handled by block coordinate descent. Online, each DU's problem becomes an MDP whose state is how far the previous DU ran past this one's start, s = max(y_(i−1) − t_i, 0); the value function is learned with linear features, and the price is updated on a slower time scale.
- **Results.** +6% (independent DUs) and +28% (interdependent DUs) over a myopic scheme; on Coastguard, 29.9 against 27.1 dB; the online algorithm about 0.5 dB below the full-knowledge solution.
- **Verdict.** [DP]: MED. It is a RaDiO-style per-unit decomposition with a resource price, for one stream. There is no multi-user coupling, no index policy and no bound beyond the duality gap of a convex program. The report's multi-user verdict rests on 0903.0207's abstract until that paper is read.

### 3. Yu, Xu and Tong, "Deadline Scheduling as Restless Bandits," IEEE TAC 63(8):2343–2358, Aug. 2018

- **Setting.** Infinite-horizon discounted (β = 0.999 in the experiments). N queue positions (arms) and M processors, Σa ≤ M, turned into an equality with M dummy arms. A job's state is (T, B): lead time to the deadline and remaining work. A processing cost c[t] follows an exogenous Markov chain shared by all arms; the reward is 1 − c per unit processed, with a convex non-completion penalty F(B) at the deadline. Each position receives i.i.d. arrivals Q(T, B).
- **Results.** Every arm is indexable (Thm 1, an elementary proof that includes arrivals). For a constant cost the Whittle index has a closed form (Thm 2): 0 if B = 0; 1 − c₀ if 1 ≤ B ≤ T − 1; β^(T−1)[F(B − T + 1) − F(B − T)] + 1 − c₀ if B ≥ T. Less laxity means higher priority. For M < N no index policy is optimal in general (Prop. 1, a three-job counterexample). The gap bound (Lemma 1) is a CVaR-type tail, G − G_W ≤ C/(1 − β)·E[I | I > M]·P(I > M), proved by comparing with the uncapacitated policy (M = N), not with a Lagrangian bound. Asymptotics (Thm 3): with Poisson arrivals of mean μ, the Whittle policy is asymptotically optimal as M → ∞ if μ < M/e, with gap O(μe^(−μ)/√M). **That is the light-traffic regime, where the capacity rarely binds.** An LLLP enhancement (less laxity and longer processing time first, by a stable topological sort of a dominance DAG) adds about 10%. Under hard deadlines (F(B) = 10B), LLSP (smaller remaining work first) raises the completion ratio.
- **Numerics.** The plotted "Upper Bound" is Whittle's average-constraint relaxation ("replacing the strict capacity limit by the constraint on the average"), shown beside EDF, LLF, Whittle and Whittle+LLLP at N = 10–50 (Figs. 3–6). The gap is shown against M at N = 1000 (Fig. 7).
- **Definitions that matter to us.** Def. 2 takes the Whittle index as the infimum subsidy at which the passive action is optimal. Where taking and leaving are worth the same over a range of prices, that is the **bottom** of the range; our [WI] takes the top.
- **Verdict.** [M]: the problem twin of `tools/stream_sim.py` (arrivals, a deadline per job, an arm per job), as the report says. Two of its sentences need fixing. "Its gap vanishes with scale" holds only in light traffic; our B = K/8 frames are heavy traffic, where the slot budget always binds and Hao, Xu and Tong's theorem is the relevant one. And their numerical bound is the single-price Whittle relaxation, not per-slot prices. The LLSP result is a published cousin of our shortest-remaining-need admission at the target.

### 4. Hao, Xu and Tong, "Asymptotically Optimal Lagrangian Priority Policy for Deadline Scheduling with Processing Rate Limits," IEEE TAC, accepted version (DOI 10.1109/TAC.2021.3049340), 15 pp.

- **Setting.** Finite horizon T. I processors (arms), at most m_t active at stage t, with m_t time-varying and deterministic. Job state (d, e): deadline and remaining demand. The processing cost c_t is exogenous and Markov; the reward is 1 − c_t per unit, with a convex quadratic penalty g(e) at the deadline; arrivals are random. The preliminary version appeared at ACC 2020.
- **Relaxation.** Lagrangian relaxation with one multiplier per stage, λ_t (Hawkins). The dual min over λ ≥ 0 of Σ_i V_1^i(λ) + Σ_t λ_t m_t is an upper bound on the optimum (eq. 12). They **solve it exactly as a linear program** (PLP, eq. 13; its dual DLP over occupation measures, eq. 15), which gives λ* and the relaxation's optimal randomized policy φ*(1|x) = ρ*(x,1)/(ρ*(x,0) + ρ*(x,1)) (eq. 16).
- **The index.** Abstract: "we define the Lagrangian priority value as the greatest tax under which it is optimal to activate the arm". Eq. (18) takes the sup over λ_t of the taxes at which the two actions are equally attractive, **with the future multipliers fixed at the relaxation's optimum λ*_(t+1..T)**, unlike a Whittle index, which uses one constant subsidy over the rest of the horizon (their eq. 24). Thm 1 gives the closed form v = r(1) + E[V_(t+1) | a = 1] − r(0) − E[V_(t+1) | a = 0]: the advantage of acting at the optimal future prices. The activation gain falls with the current tax at slope exactly −1, so the indifference point is unique (App. A), and the sup is notational. Thm 2 gives a recursion for deterministic costs. Remark 2 distinguishes it from Brown and Smith's Lagrangian index, which is defined under any feasible multiplier, and points to similar values in Hu and Frazier for a time-invariant limit.
- **The policy and the tie rule.** Sort by v and activate up to m_t arms with non-negative values (work-conserving). Ties are broken by randomisation (Def. 1): v + δ with probability φ*(1|x) (0.5 for states φ* never visits), v − δ otherwise, then uniformly. Example 1 (T = 4, m_t/I = 1/4) has two states with the same v = 1 at t = 3 that the relaxation activates with probabilities 1/4 and 1/2, so uniform tie-breaking is asymptotically suboptimal there. Remark 3: Hu and Frazier split ties in proportion to unconditional occupation measures and round fractional actions; Hao, Xu and Tong use conditional probabilities and avoid rounding.
- **Theorem 3.** With deterministic costs and β copies of every arm (capacity βm_t), (βL* − J^π)/β → 0 as β → ∞, in light and heavy traffic. The proof's Cauchy–Schwarz step suggests an O(1/√β) rate per arm, which the paper does not state.
- **Numerics.** EV charging with PJM prices and CAISO capacity, T = 24 four-hour stages, I = 50 to 4000, 5000 runs per point. Heavy overload (E m_t/I = 0.205): the policy with the randomized tie rule converges to the bound. It beats uniform tie-breaking by 6–8% and the Whittle index by 22–24%, and Hu and Frazier's index heuristic by 5.9% shrinking to 2.2% from I = 50 to 200. Slight overload (0.195, harsher penalty): 45–49% over Whittle (uniform ties: 33–36% over Whittle), and 4.9% shrinking to 1.9% over Hu and Frazier. **In Figs. 3–4 the uniform-tie and Whittle curves stay flat below the bound as I grows to 4000: a conflict rule other than the relaxation's leaves a per-arm gap that does not shrink.** Most of the margin over Whittle comes from time-varying capacity, which a constant subsidy cannot see.
- **Relation to ours.** Our [LI] (`schedule_sim.py` and `rmab_scale.py --policies lagrange`) is their Lagrangian priority policy applied to our arm: the gain of taking the slot in each user's program priced at the bound's per-slot prices (v − λ*_t, the same ranking within a slot), work-conserving, without indexability. Two things differ. Our prices come from the best constant price plus 10 projected-subgradient steps, not an exact LP. Our ties, within the programs' precision, go to the Whittle index, not to φ*. Our capacity per slot is constant, so a small margin over Whittle is what their analysis predicts: we measure +2.8 points at 30 dB at K 8.
- **Verdict.** [LI]: HIGH, by name and definition. The paper should present the Lagrangian index as their policy (with Hu and Frazier, Brown and Smith), not as ours. Two report statements are corrected. The verbatim definition is "the greatest tax under which it is optimal to activate the arm" (the report's quotation was a paraphrase). And they do not establish our top-of-range convention in any substantive sense: under fixed future prices the range is a single point, while Yu, Xu and Tong's Whittle index takes the bottom. The convention remains our implementation choice, worth one sentence.
- **What it says about our 30 dB gap.** The report flagged that our Lagrangian index stays about 1.5 points below the bound at every K (B = K/8). This paper is the first published instance of that symptom, with its cause: ties that the relaxation would randomize, broken by another rule. In our frames with fresh reports the decision SNR is continuous, so exact ties are rare. They are structural only where states coincide: useless or stale reports, and the flat regions a 0/1 reward creates. The price solver is the other suspect, and a check run on 2026-10-20 found a weakness there. `alloc/whittle.lagrangian_bound` scales its subgradient step with the unit imbalance, which grows with B, so at B = 4 and 8 (the two sizes checked) the per-slot refinement never improved on the constant price. On the smoke stand-in (8 images, P = 6, T = 24, one draw per K, mean objective), K 8 / 32 / 64 give 31.9393 / 31.3366 / 31.6951 dB per user at the constant price. Sixty unscaled steps give 31.9319 / 31.3366 / 31.6951; sixty steps with the step divided by the supply give 31.9319 / 31.3302 / 31.6882. For mean PSNR the constant price is nearly optimal (0.007 dB per user), which fits the 0.005 dB gap measured at K 128. The stand-in's 30 dB target does not bind (all optimal prices are 0), so the target case needs the real utility. Diagnostic for a later COMMANDS section: supply-scaled steps and more of them; a φ*-style randomized tie rule; a bound with finer fade bins; and runs on the programs' own discrete channel, where their theorem applies exactly.

### 5. Chou and Miao, "Rate-Distortion Optimized Streaming of Packetized Media," IEEE TMM 8(2):390–404, Apr. 2006 (MSR-TR-2001-35)

- **Confirms** the report's account. Each data unit has its own MDP over its transmission opportunities, priced by a Lagrange multiplier. Units are coupled by iterative sensitivity adjustment (ISA), a Gauss–Seidel descent to a local optimum. The rate-controlled variant sends the unit with the largest threshold multiplier λ_l. Its closed-form λ_l costs "very little penalty (a fraction of a dB)" and runs at about 1% of a 700 MHz CPU. The bound is the operational D(R) at erasure capacity (System 5). Gains: up to 4 dB over omniscient retransmission and 6 dB or more over no error control, at 20% loss.
- **Adds four details.**
  - Feedback is delayed (ACKs over a random round trip, shifted-Gamma delays) and handled inside each unit's MDP over action–observation histories (Fig. 4). A per-unit program acting on delayed information is therefore old, though the information is about delivery, not the channel.
  - With feedback, ISA is re-run at every opportunity, conditioned on the latest acknowledgements, and only the first action is taken (stepwise re-solving). The authors liken it to agents who each optimise their own long-term strategy "assuming an expected level of success for each other agent".
  - Rate control re-sets λ at every opportunity so that exactly one unit is sent. That is a per-opportunity clearing price chosen greedily, not prices optimised jointly over the horizon, so the report's mapping row "Lagrange multiplier λ ↔ per-slot price" needs that qualifier.
  - The closed form is λ_l ≈ 0 for units within one forward trip of their deadline, already acknowledged, or sent within the last round trip; otherwise λ_l ≈ S_l/B_l, times ε_F/ε_R for each unacknowledged transmission.
- **Verdict** unchanged: earliest ancestor of [DP] + [WI]-type selection in media. Add the delayed-feedback point to the stale-report discussion: delayed feedback inside a per-unit program is old; a channel-report kernel inside a deadline program with the bound intact was not found.

### 6. McDowell, Panebianco, Chakraborty and Sun, "Significance-Driven Semantic Communication," arXiv 2608.28441v2 (31 Aug 2026), 18 pp., Auburn University

- **Setting.** N edge sensors (traffic cameras) send to one receiver over an orthogonal multiple-access channel, i.i.d. Rayleigh block fading per slot. Each slot the receiver gives each sensor a codelength η ∈ {0, m, …, Km} complex symbols, with Σ η ≤ W. A sensor not served leaves the receiver with its most recent latent, whose age of information (AoI) grows. The objective is infinite-horizon discounted "semantic significance": the conditional value of sampled information of the freshest latent, an L-divergence D_L(P_{Y|S=s} ∥ P_Y) (Prop. 3.1), computed from the decoder's own posterior. Data: four live traffic cameras at one intersection, pedestrian-safety labels from YOLOv8s + DeepSORT, an asymmetric loss.
- **Physical layer (Meta-VIB).** A BiLSTM encoder and a residual MLP decoder, both modulated by FiLM from a hypernetwork fed (β, η, ζ): the multiplier of the information constraint, the codelength and the instantaneous SNR. CSI is at both ends; the decoder equalises with h. The latent is truncated to its first η coordinates, and a hinge regulariser keeps the posterior log-variances non-decreasing, so the prefix carries the most information: a learned prefix code within one slot. Trained in three phases over SNR −5 to 20 dB and AoI 0 to 500 slots, then frozen. The physical-layer baselines (JSCC, DeepJSCC, VIB, Hyper-VIB, ATROC) are trained at 10 dB and AoI 0 with codelength 2.
- **MAC layer (Q-Maximization).** The per-slot budget is relaxed to a discounted one, with one multiplier λ found by bisection. Each sensor's MDP has reward g_n(ω, η) − λη. Its Q-function is learned by a λ-conditioned actor-critic (PPO), because the state (latent, AoI, SNR) is continuous. Each slot a multiple-choice knapsack is solved by DP with an LP-priority tie rule: among tied maximisers, keep the larger codelength. They attribute to a manuscript in preparation (Chakraborty, Sun, Hou and Eryilmaz, 2026) the result that the same knapsack with arbitrary ties is not asymptotically optimal, while the LP-priority version is. Appendix C states the fluid LP upper bound; no figure plots it (Fig. 4's "theoretical upper bound" is the physical layer's H_L(Y)).
- **Results.** Up to 1000× semantic spectrum efficiency at 0 dB and 40× at 5 dB, against DeepJSCC paired with greedy, max-age or round-robin scheduling (baselines trained at one SNR, which explains much of the margin). Q-Maximization beats net-gain maximisation (NGM, Shisher et al.) on the same Meta-VIB. Training took 72 h for Meta-VIB and 3.5 h for Q-Maximization on one RTX PRO 6000 GPU.
- **Verdict.** (7): LOW, as the report said. The physical layer is trained over a broad exogenous SNR range and frozen before the scheduler is learned, so it never sees the scheduler's channel; "jointly optimizes" means one objective with the layers solved one after the other. (2a): HIGH on the method (Lagrangian relaxation, per-sensor programs, an LP-priority policy), with an infinite horizon, one price, and a bound stated but not computed. Setting: **the nearest learned-code scheduler found so far**. It also corrects SURVEY F2's remark that semantic RMABs use abstract rewards: theirs is decoded. What it lacks is our arm. Every slot carries a fresh, self-contained codeword of the newest observation, so nothing accumulates across slots; the passive arm decays with AoI instead of freezing; there is no deadline; CSI is perfect. And the value of a slot is learned by RL, because no link abstraction compresses the state. That last point deserves a sentence in the paper: our abstraction turns the same problem class into exact programs on a two-number state, solved on a CPU.

### 7. Shisher, Ji, Hou and Sun, "Learning and Communications Co-Design for Remote Inference Systems: Feature Length Selection and Transmission Scheduling," IEEE JSAIT 4:524–538, 2023 (arXiv 2308.10094v3, 24 Jun 2024), 18 pp.

- **Setting.** Remote inference. A sensor's buffer holds its B most recent observations, and the transmitter sends a feature of length l starting at position b ("selection-from-buffer"). The inference error err(δ, l) of a trained predictor depends on the AoI δ and the length l: non-increasing in l (Lemma 1), not necessarily monotonic in δ (experiments: CSI prediction under a Jakes model, and CartPole). Single source: an infinite-horizon average-cost semi-MDP, with a closed-form threshold policy for a fixed length (Thm 1) and a Bellman equation solved by policy iteration for varying lengths (Thm 2).
- **Multiple sources.** M source–predictor pairs share N channels; a feature of length l uses l channels for one slot, Σ l_j ≤ N. The per-slot budget is relaxed to a time-average one, which gives a lower bound on the original problem. Lagrangian dual decomposition yields M per-source average-cost MDPs with cost λl, and λ* comes from stochastic subgradient ascent with step β/k. The **net gain** (eqs. 64–65) is α(δ, d, l) = h(δ+1, d) − h(b̂(l)+1, l) − λl: the advantage of sending length l over not sending, at λ*. Each slot a bounded knapsack is solved by DP in O(MNB); no indexability is needed. During revision they found a similar gain index for two actions (Chen and Liew, IEEE TIT 2023, uncertainty of information).
- **The bound.** Fig. 8 plots the policy's average inference error beside the relaxation's lower bound as sources and channels grow together (M = 3r, N = 10r, r = 1 to 10). The gap nearly closes by r = 3 (read from the plot).
- **Channel.** No fading or SNR: a channel is a resource unit; a feature takes one slot in the multi-source model and ⌈αl⌉ slots in the single-source simulations. The receiver holds B predictors, one per feature length, trained offline.
- **Verdict.** (2a): HIGH, the method twin the report described, and the "net gain" label is now confirmed from the text. It is the Lagrangian index (the advantage at λ*), multi-action, average cost, one constant price. **A bound-versus-scale plot exists here too**, so the kind of evidence in our scaling study is not new (batch 1 already found Hao, Xu and Tong's I = 50 to 4000). (7): LOW, confirmed: a fixed menu of predictors, not a code trained on the scheduler's channel. (6): its B predictors, one per length, are the remote-inference cousin of a decoder per length.

### 8. Kurka and Gündüz, "Bandwidth-Agile Image Transmission with Deep Joint Source-Channel Coding," arXiv 2009.12480v2 (14 Jun 2021; IEEE TWC 2021), 32 pp., Imperial College London

- **Setting.** An image sent in L layers, each over an independent channel realisation (AWGN or slow Rayleigh): successive refinement (the first i layers) and multiple descriptions (any subset). CIFAR-10 with the original DeepJSCC CNN; Kodak with a larger GDN network trained on ImageNet.
- **Architectures.** (a) One encoder and L decoders, decoder i reading the first i layers: **one encoder with a decoder per prefix length**. (b) One decoder trained with random tail masking, the number of layers drawn per batch ("structured dropout"); training the lengths one after another hurt the first layers. (c) Residual transmission: a new encoder–decoder pair per layer, the transmitter simulating m = 10 channel realisations to estimate the receiver's reconstruction. All three perform alike on CIFAR-10 (Figs. 4 and 10).
- **Results.** On CIFAR-10 layering costs almost nothing (L = 2, L = 5, and up to L = 16 at a total ratio of 1/3). On Kodak with the larger network (Fig. 8, read from the plot), two layers of 1/12 trail one transmission of 1/6 by about 0.4 dB at 1 dB SNR, growing to about 1.7 dB at 25 dB; the base layer matches single transmission. Against JPEG2000 or BPG with a capacity-achieving code it is better at low SNR and for the first layers. Loss weights matter little except at the extremes (App. A).
- **SNR mismatch (Sec. IV-B, Fig. 5).** Models trained at one SNR are tested over a range, with **one common test SNR for both layers** (AWGN); for slow Rayleigh fading only the average SNR is reported. Below the training SNR the refinement layer's gain shrinks, "since the reconstructed base layer becomes significantly different from what the encoder expects based on the training data". Remark 1: the base reconstruction depends on the channel realisation, so a refinement layer cannot simply send the residual.
- **Verdict.** (6): the mechanism of C6 is theirs exactly. Multiple-decoder DeepJSCC-l is encoding once with a decoder per length, and its single decoder is our prefix training, so C6 stays a system verdict. Their CIFAR-10 tie between the two architectures differs from our measurement that a decoder per length recovers about half the nesting penalty at equal budget. Their Kodak curve shows the penalty appearing with a larger network (0.4 to 1.7 dB), comparable to our 0.17 to 0.47 dB and JSCCformer-f's 0.58 to 1.73 dB. (1)/C12: no per-layer SNR profiles and no composition rule. But Sec. IV-B and Remark 1 give the mechanism behind C12's finding that a code trained at constant SNR obeys no rule: refinements are coded against the base reconstruction expected at the training SNR. This answers the report's question about this paper: layers are never tested at different SNRs, and the degradation claim holds below the training SNR.

### 9. Pan, Li, Zhang, Cai and Yu, "Deep Refinement-Based Joint Source Channel Coding over Time-Varying Channels," arXiv 2311.15309v1 (26 Nov 2023), 6 pp., Zhejiang University (venue not stated)

- **Method.** The code is split into m blocks sent in sequence. A static encoder codes the image with the first CSI h₀; whenever the gain changes, a dynamic encoder re-encodes the blocks not yet sent with the new CSI. Its RC module has a weight correction λ ∈ (0, 1), set to 0 when the channel improves, followed by dispersing, reconstruction with attention-feature modules, aggregating and a residual integration. Instantaneous CSI is fed back, and the decoder also sees h.
- **Training.** CIFAR-10; R = 1/12 with 8 blocks (R = 1/6 with 16 blocks for Rayleigh). **The SNR is drawn from U[0, 20] dB for every block** (confirmed); the baseline ADJSCC gets one SNR per image.
- **Results.** At constant SNR it is on par with ADJSCC and about 1 dB above fixed-SNR deep JSCC at that model's training SNR (Fig. 5). For given per-block SNR profiles (2, 3 or 8 segments, Fig. 6), DRJSCC stays flat. ADJSCC, fed one SNR, matches it only when the SNR barely changes, and otherwise trails by up to about 2 dB even at its best input SNR (read from the plots). Over Rayleigh fading (12 drawn scenarios of 128 images, Fig. 7) DRJSCC leads ADJSCC by about 1.5 to 11 dB per scenario (read from the plot), more when the gain changes on every block.
- **Verdict.** (1): it does give PSNR for given per-block SNR profiles (the report's open question), but for a re-encoding scheme with CSI feedback, and with no rule that composes the blocks. Its Fig. 6 is a second published observation that a code trained with one SNR per image degrades when the SNR changes within the image. (6): re-encoding, not encode-once. (7): an exogenous U[0, 20] dB channel. No verdict changes.
