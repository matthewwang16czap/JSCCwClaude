\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{D}{eep} joint source-channel coding (JSCC) can map an image directly to channel symbols
with a single learned encoder and decoder, eliminating the separate source and channel codes that
digital coding needs. Trained end to end through the channel, deep JSCC beats digital transmission
at low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and degrades gracefully as the channel worsens, instead of
failing once the SNR drops below what a digital code was designed for \cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Gunduz23}.
That makes it a strong candidate for image delivery in 6G, yet a learned code is designed and
evaluated on a single link at a given SNR. A cellular downlink is a multi-user, multi-slot system
with fading, imperfect channel knowledge and deadlines, so the gain a learned code reports on a
single link \cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Xu22,Yang25} is reduced when a base station
delivers to many users over fading slots. A base station splits a frame of slots among many users
through a scheduler, and each user's slots fade independently. Moreover, the base station knows
only an estimate of the current slot's channel, making a channel-quality report \cite{TS36213}
often late or wrong. Since each delivery has a deadline, a low-latency scheduling method is 
necessary as well.

Existing approaches to serving images to many users over a fading downlink fall into two
categories, namely \emph{physical link design} and \emph{JSCC design}. The scheduler of the former
serves the bits of a separate image codec, whereas that of the latter serves a learned code.

Physical link design is how cellular systems deliver images today. As illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(a), its scheduler selects both the served users and the rate of their
blocks in every slot. Link adaptation sets each rate from the channel quality indicator (CQI),
hybrid automatic repeat request (HARQ) retransmits failed blocks, and proportional fair (PF)
scheduling serves each user near the peak of its channel quality
\cite{Goldsmith97,Caire01,Viswanath02}. These designs assume that each block is either decoded 
or lost and produce the cliff effect, in which a slot below the design SNR of its block delivers 
nothing and any SNR above it adds no quality \cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Gunduz23}. Moreover, link 
adaptation and HARQ protect the bits from imperfect channel knowledge only by spending extra slots.

In contrast, JSCC design constructs the downlink on a learned code, whose distortion varies 
continuously with the SNR of its symbols. Hence no transmission is lost outright, and no coding rate 
has to be matched to an uncertain report. On a single link, per-image rate selection sends each 
image at the least rate at which its predicted quality meets a target \cite{Zhang23}. As illustrated 
in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(b), multi-user schedulers in this category value each user by such a 
predicted quality rather than by bits. They allocate the shared channel by the quality predicted 
at each user's SNR \cite{Yan22,Xia24,Hou26}, train an allocation policy by deep reinforcement 
learning \cite{Wang22,Zhang23t}, or embed such learning in a restless bandit over fading slots
\cite{McDowell26} (Section~\ref{sec:related-jscc}).

Although JSCC design avoids the cliff effect of physical link design, it leaves three challenges
unresolved:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Absence of a link abstraction}: The quality models of JSCC design, whether fitted curves
or learned predictors \cite{Yan22,Zhang23,Hou26}, take a single SNR as input. Each model maps this
SNR to the predicted quality of a whole image, as if every symbol of the image met the same channel.
Physical link design
provides rules for the error rate of a digital block, namely the exponential and mutual-information
effective SNR mappings (EESM and MIESM) \cite{Ericsson03,EMD09}, but no rule is known for how the
SNRs of the slots that carry one image combine into the quality of a nonlinear code.
\item \emph{Absence of tractable and certifiable scheduling}: Choosing the users of every slot up to the
deadline, without knowledge of future slots, is a stochastic dynamic program whose state grows
exponentially with the number of users. The quality-based allocations are myopic, since they ignore
the slots that remain in the frame \cite{Yan22,Xia24,Hou26}. Learned policies
\cite{Wang22,Zhang23t,McDowell26} require many training interactions and offer no certificate of
their distance from the optimum.
\item \emph{Imperfect information}: The scheduler makes decisions based on predictions of both the
quality and the channel, both of which are imperfect. Per-image rate selection \cite{Zhang23}
treats its predicted quality as exact, so a user predicted just above a quality target may decode
just below it. The learned restless-bandit scheduler likewise assumes exact channel knowledge
\cite{McDowell26}. Moreover, the advantage that motivates these designs was measured against an
idealized digital link with exact channel knowledge and capacity-achieving codes
\cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Kurka20}. Whether this advantage persists under the reports of a real base
station remains open.
\end{itemize}

The first challenge underlies the other two, since a scheduler needs a link abstraction both to plan
over the remaining slots with a certificate and to account for prediction errors. We therefore ask
whether the decoded quality of a learned code can be predicted from its slot SNRs at all, and how
the code should be trained to make this possible. To answer these questions, we trained the same
progressive architecture in two ways, under slot-varying SNR and at one constant SNR per image. We
then compared candidate rules on both codes and evaluated the most accurate rule within a scheduler
(Sections~\ref{sec:method-abstraction}, \ref{sec:eval-abstraction} and~\ref{sec:eval-ablation}).
Three findings emerged. First, the code trained under slot-varying SNR has an additive link
abstraction, a property that linear analog codes have by construction \cite{Jakubczak11}. A source
term plus a weighted sum of per-slot minimum mean-square-error (MMSE) terms predicts its decoded
quality more accurately than EESM and MIESM. Second, the training condition determines whether the
abstraction holds. For the code trained at one constant SNR per image, none of the candidate rules
predicts the decoded quality with comparable accuracy. Third, this abstraction can be calibrated
from a small number of decodes per image. Within a scheduler, it then predicts the quality of
complete deliveries accurately, even with noisy or aged channel reports. The remaining prediction
error is small but not negligible, since a scheduler that ignores it leaves more users below a
quality target. These findings show that a learned code can be scheduled through its link
abstraction, provided that the scheduler models this error.

To address these challenges, we propose CLASP (Cal\-i\-bra\-tion-aware, Link-Ab\-strac\-tion-based Scheduling
of Progressive codes), a scheduler for learned progressive codes in a multi-user fading downlink.
Unlike existing JSCC methods, which value each user at one SNR or learn the allocation by
reinforcement learning, CLASP values every slot for every user through the code's link abstraction and plans each
user's delivery to the deadline exactly. It has one design for each challenge.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Link abstraction.} The progressive code is trained under slot-varying SNR and its MMSE
link abstraction is calibrated per image, so that each user becomes a two-number state.
\item \emph{Scheduler.} On this state the frame becomes a finite-horizon restless bandit
\cite{Whittle88}, a model already used to schedule layered video \cite{Hosseini19} and learned
semantic codes \cite{McDowell26}. A Lagrangian relaxation splits it into one small dynamic program per user under
per-slot prices, solved exactly on a CPU; the relaxation's value is an upper bound on every causal
scheduler; and users are served in the order of the Lagrangian index those programs define
\cite{Hao22,Brown20}.
\item \emph{Imperfect information.} Each program rewards the probability of reaching the quality
target under a calibrated predictive distribution of the user's decoded quality, rather than a hard
threshold on the predicted quality. The distribution's spread is the measured error of the
prediction, from the calibrated curves and from the channel reports. Given an aged report, each
program decides on the posterior of the channel, not on the report itself.
\end{itemize}

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Discovery.} We find that a learned progressive code trained under slot-varying SNR obeys
an additive MMSE link abstraction, more accurate than EESM and MIESM, while a code trained at
constant SNR obeys none, and that a transmitter can calibrate it from 12 decodes per image. To our
knowledge, effective-SNR mappings had not been tested on a learned code before.
\item \emph{Method.} We propose CLASP, which casts multi-user deadline scheduling of learned codes
as a finite-horizon restless bandit with a two-number user state, solves it with exact per-user
programs, a Lagrangian bound and an index policy, and is robust to the errors of its own quality
prediction and of the channel reports.
\item \emph{Performance.} Decoded by the codec, CLASP gains 0.5--0.6~dB of mean PSNR over PF and
brings an extra 8--15\% of the users to 30~dB over per-image rate selection, with fresh, noisy or
aged reports. In simulation its gap to the bound vanishes as the cell grows: from 0.21 to
0.005~dB per user between 8 and 128 users for mean PSNR, and for the target within $\pm 0.3\%$ of
the bound from 16 users on. Against a digital chain with CQI-table link adaptation and HARQ, it leads
at every CSI condition tested, by 1.2--1.6~dB and an extra 13--16\% of the users at 30~dB with
2~dB CQI errors or reports aged to correlation 0.8. An idealized digital code 3~dB from capacity
with the same HARQ leads CLASP by 2.4\% of the users with exact reports and trails it by 5--14\% from
1~dB CQI errors or correlation 0.95 on. Training the code on the channel CLASP creates adds
0.21~dB, decoded.
\end{itemize}
