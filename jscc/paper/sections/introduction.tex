\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{D}{eep} joint source-channel coding (JSCC) can map an image directly to channel symbols
with a single learned encoder and decoder, eliminating the separate source and channel codes that
digital coding needs. Trained end to end through the channel, deep JSCC beats digital transmission
at low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and degrades gracefully as the channel worsens, instead of
failing once the SNR drops below what a digital code was designed for
\cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Gunduz23}. That makes it a strong candidate for image delivery in 6G, yet a
learned code is designed and evaluated on a single link at a given SNR. A cellular downlink is a
multi-user, multi-slot system with fading, imperfect channel knowledge and deadlines, so the gain a
learned code reports on a single link \cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Xu22,Yang25} is not automatically the
gain a base station delivers to many users over fading slots. A base station splits a frame of
slots among many users through a scheduler, and each user's slots fade independently. Moreover, the
base station knows only an estimate of the current slot's channel, a channel-quality report
\cite{TS36213} that is often late or wrong. Since each delivery has a deadline, such errors matter
all the more.

Existing approaches to serving images to many users over a fading downlink fall into two categories
(Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}): \emph{physical link design}, which keeps the image codec separate and
engineers the link that carries its bits, and \emph{the JSCC way}, which lets a learned code take on
the channel itself.

Physical link design is how cellular systems deliver images today: a standard codec compresses the
image into bits once, and the physical layer carries them with link adaptation from the user's
channel quality indicator (CQI) reports, hybrid automatic repeat request (HARQ), and a
channel-aware scheduler such as proportional fair (PF) \cite{Goldsmith97,Caire01,Viswanath02}. Its
tools for many users are mature (Section~\ref{sec:related-link}), but they rest on a binary outcome
per block, decoded or lost, so a user's quality is a step function of its channel: a slot below
the block's design SNR delivers nothing, and SNR above it is wasted, the cliff effect
\cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Gunduz23}. Moreover, the rate is committed before the slot is seen, from a
report that may be late or wrong, and link adaptation and HARQ protect the bits only by spending
extra slots. In our simulations, going from fresh reports to aged ones (correlation 0.8) costs the
digital chain 1.9~dB of mean peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), against 0.6~dB for a learned code
under our scheduler (Section~\ref{sec:eval-csi}).

In contrast, the JSCC way replaces the two codes with one learned code that maps the image straight
to channel symbols \cite{Bourtsoulatze19}. Its quality degrades smoothly with the SNR its symbols
meet, so no block is lost outright and no coding rate has to be matched to an uncertain report; the
same symbols serve receivers of different SNRs, as linear analog codes showed for multicast video
\cite{Jakubczak11}. Most of this work adapts a single link to the channel and the budget
(Section~\ref{sec:related-jscc}). To serve many users, the JSCC way splits the shared channel by each
user's predicted quality at its SNR \cite{Yan22,Xia24,Hou26}, or learns the allocation by deep
reinforcement learning \cite{Wang22,Zhang23t}, most recently inside a restless bandit over fading
slots \cite{McDowell26}. Our method belongs to the JSCC way: it schedules a learned code to many
users across fading slots.

Although existing JSCC methods are effective, they face three challenges when a base station
schedules a learned code to many users over fading slots.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{No link abstraction.} They value a user's transmission at a single SNR, with a fitted
quality-SNR curve or a learned quality predictor \cite{Yan22,Zhang23,Hou26}. A scheduled image,
however, is spread over slots of different SNRs, and for a nonlinear learned code no rule is known
for how these SNRs combine into decoded quality. The exponential and the mutual-information
effective SNR mappings (EESM, MIESM) of physical link design predict a digital block's error rate
from the SNRs of the resources it occupies \cite{Ericsson03,EMD09}, not a learned code's
distortion, and to our knowledge have not been validated on a learned code.
\item \emph{No tractable, certifiable scheduler.} Deciding which user each slot serves, before a
deadline and without knowing future slots, is a stochastic dynamic program whose state grows
exponentially with the number of users. Existing methods either decide each transmission at its
current SNR without planning over the slots left before the deadline \cite{Yan22,Xia24,Hou26}, or
learn the allocation by reinforcement learning, which needs many interactions to train and gives no
certificate of how far the learned policy is from optimal \cite{Wang22,Zhang23t,McDowell26}.
\item \emph{Imperfect information.} The quality model and the channel reports are both estimates,
yet existing schedulers take them at face value: per-image rate selection (PADC \cite{Zhang23})
treats its predicted quality as exact, so a user predicted just above a quality target can decode
just below it, and the closest learned scheduler assumes exact channel knowledge \cite{McDowell26}.
Moreover, the advantage of the JSCC way over physical link design was measured on single links
against an idealized digital side, with exact channel knowledge and capacity-achieving codes
\cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Kurka20}; whether it survives the reports a real base station has is open.
\end{itemize}
Behind all three lies one question: does a learned code have a link abstraction at all, a rule that,
like EESM for a digital block, turns the SNRs its symbols met into its decoded quality? And if it
does, what decides whether a code obeys it?

To answer it, we trained a progressive code, which decodes from any prefix of its phases, under
slot-varying SNR, and tested candidate rules on held-out decodes with mixed slot SNRs
(Sections~\ref{sec:method-abstraction} and~\ref{sec:eval-abstraction}). We found three things.
First, its distortion adds up over slots: the source distortion plus a sensitivity-weighted sum of
per-slot minimum mean-square-error (MMSE) terms predicts held-out decodes with a mean absolute error
of 0.055--0.083~dB on two test sets, against 0.16--0.19~dB for EESM and 0.29--0.34~dB for MIESM with
the same per-phase weights. Linear analog codes obey this form by construction \cite{Jakubczak11};
here a nonlinear learned code acquires it. Second, training decides it: the same architecture
trained at one constant SNR per image obeys no such rule (the MMSE rule errs by 0.22~dB, and no rule
does better than 0.15~dB), because it codes its later phases against the base reconstruction it
expects at one SNR, the mismatch DeepJSCC-l observed for layers \cite{Kurka21}. Third, the
abstraction is cheap and accurate, yet its small error matters. Twelve decodes per image calibrate
it, and it predicts the decoded quality of complete scheduled deliveries with a mean absolute error
of 0.06--0.09~dB and a bias within $\pm 0.03$~dB, with exact, noisy or aged reports. At a hard
quality target, however, a scheduler that takes the calibrated curves as exact leaves an extra
1.7--2.0\% of the users below 30~dB, at every cell size from 16 to 128 users. In short, training
gives a learned code a link abstraction. With it, each user's state reduces to two numbers, the
phases received and their accumulated weighted noise, and scheduling becomes a planning problem,
provided the abstraction's residual error is part of the objective.

To address these challenges, we propose CLASP (Cal\-i\-bra\-tion-aware, Link-Ab\-strac\-tion-based Scheduling
of Progressive codes), a scheduler for learned progressive codes in a multi-user fading downlink.
Unlike existing JSCC methods, which value each user at one SNR or learn the allocation by
reinforcement learning, CLASP values every slot for every user through the code's link abstraction and plans each
user's delivery to the deadline exactly. It has one design for each challenge.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Link abstraction.} The progressive code is trained under slot-varying SNR and its MMSE
link abstraction is calibrated per image, so that each user becomes a two-number state.
\item \emph{Scheduler.} On this state the frame becomes a finite-horizon restless bandit
\cite{Whittle88}, a model already used to schedule layered video \cite{Hosseini19} and learned
semantic codes \cite{McDowell26}. A Lagrangian relaxation splits it into one small dynamic program per user under
per-slot prices, solved exactly on a CPU; the relaxation's value is an upper bound on every causal
scheduler; and users are served in the order of the Lagrangian index those programs define
\cite{Hao22,Brown20}.
\item \emph{Imperfect information.} Each program rewards the probability of reaching the quality
target under a calibrated predictive distribution of the user's decoded quality, rather than a hard
threshold on the predicted quality. The distribution's spread is the measured error of the
prediction, from the calibrated curves and from the channel reports. Given an aged report, each
program decides on the posterior of the channel, not on the report itself.
\end{itemize}

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Discovery.} We find that a learned progressive code trained under slot-varying SNR obeys
an additive MMSE link abstraction, more accurate than EESM and MIESM, while a code trained at
constant SNR obeys none, and that a transmitter can calibrate it from 12 decodes per image. To our
knowledge, effective-SNR mappings had not been tested on a learned code before.
\item \emph{Method.} We propose CLASP, which casts multi-user deadline scheduling of learned codes
as a finite-horizon restless bandit with a two-number user state, solves it with exact per-user
programs, a Lagrangian bound and an index policy, and is robust to the errors of its own quality
prediction and of the channel reports.
\item \emph{Performance.} Decoded by the codec, CLASP gains 0.5--0.6~dB of mean PSNR over PF and
brings an extra 8--15\% of the users to 30~dB over per-image rate selection, with fresh, noisy or
aged reports. In simulation its gap to the bound vanishes as the cell grows: from 0.21 to
0.005~dB per user between 8 and 128 users for mean PSNR, and for the target within $\pm 0.3\%$ of
the bound from 16 users on. Against a digital chain with CQI-table link adaptation and HARQ, it leads
at every CSI condition tested, by 1.2--1.6~dB and an extra 13--16\% of the users at 30~dB with
2~dB CQI errors or reports aged to correlation 0.8. An idealized digital code 3~dB from capacity
with the same HARQ leads CLASP by 2.4\% of the users with exact reports and trails it by 5--14\% from
1~dB CQI errors or correlation 0.95 on. Training the code on the channel CLASP creates adds
0.21~dB, decoded.
\end{itemize}
