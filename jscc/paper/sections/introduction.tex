\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{D}{eep} joint source-channel coding (JSCC) can map an image directly to channel symbols
with a single learned encoder and decoder, eliminating the separate source and channel codes that
digital coding needs. Trained end to end through the channel, deep JSCC beats digital transmission
at low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and degrades gracefully as the channel worsens, instead of
failing once the SNR drops below what a digital code was designed for \cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Gunduz23}.
That makes it a strong candidate for image delivery in 6G, yet a learned code is designed and
evaluated on a single link at a given SNR. A cellular downlink is a multi-user, multi-slot system
with fading, imperfect channel knowledge and deadlines, so the gain a learned code reports on a
single link \cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Xu22,Yang25} is not automatically the gain a base station
delivers to many users over fading slots. A base station splits a frame of slots among many users
through a scheduler, and each user's slots fade independently. Moreover, the base station knows
only an estimate of the current slot's channel, making a channel-quality report \cite{TS36213}
often late or wrong. Since each delivery has a deadline, a low-latency scheduler is necessary as well.

Existing approaches to serving images to many users over a fading downlink fall into two
categories, namely \emph{physical link design} and \emph{the JSCC way}. The scheduler of the former
serves the bits of a separate image codec, whereas that of the latter serves a learned code.

Physical link design is how cellular systems deliver images today. As illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(a), its scheduler selects both the served users and the rate of their
blocks in every slot. Link adaptation sets each rate from the channel quality indicator (CQI),
hybrid automatic repeat request (HARQ) retransmits failed blocks, and proportional fair (PF)
scheduling serves each user near the peak of its channel quality
\cite{Goldsmith97,Caire01,Viswanath02}. Media-aware schedulers further weigh each packet by the
distortion it removes \cite{Chou06,Pahalawatta07}, and restless bandits have been applied to
layered video streaming \cite{Hosseini19} (Section~\ref{sec:related-link}). All of these designs
assume that each block is either decoded or lost. This assumption produces the cliff effect, in
which a slot below the design SNR of its block delivers nothing and any SNR above it adds no
quality \cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Gunduz23}. Moreover, link adaptation and HARQ protect the bits from
imperfect channel knowledge only by spending extra slots.

In contrast, the learned code of the JSCC way has a distortion that varies continuously with the
SNR of its symbols. Hence no transmission is lost outright, and no coding rate has to be matched
to an uncertain report. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(b), schedulers in this category
value each user by the predicted quality of the code rather than by bits. Examples include
allocating the shared channel by the quality predicted at each user's SNR \cite{Yan22,Xia24,Hou26},
training an allocation policy by deep reinforcement learning \cite{Wang22,Zhang23t}, and embedding
such learning in a restless bandit over fading slots \cite{McDowell26}
(Section~\ref{sec:related-jscc}).

Although existing JSCC methods are effective, they face three challenges in deadline scheduling
over a multi-user fading downlink.
\begin{itemize}
\item The first challenge is the absence of a \emph{link abstraction} for learned codes. The
quality models of existing methods, whether fitted curves or learned predictors
\cite{Yan22,Zhang23,Hou26}, take a single SNR as input. The symbols of a scheduled image are
instead spread over slots of different SNRs, and no rule is known for how these SNRs combine into
the quality of a nonlinear code. Physical link design provides such rules for the error rate of a
digital block, namely the exponential and mutual-information effective SNR mappings (EESM and
MIESM) \cite{Ericsson03,EMD09}. To our knowledge, neither mapping has been validated on the
distortion of a learned code.
\item The second challenge is \emph{tractable and certifiable scheduling}. Choosing the users of
every slot up to the deadline, without knowledge of future slots, is a stochastic dynamic program
whose state grows exponentially with the number of users. Existing allocations are myopic, since
they ignore the slots that remain in the frame \cite{Yan22,Xia24,Hou26}. Learned policies
\cite{Wang22,Zhang23t,McDowell26} require many training interactions and offer no certificate of
their distance from the optimum.
\item The third challenge is \emph{imperfect information}, because the scheduler decides on
predictions of both the quality and the channel. Per-image rate selection \cite{Zhang23} treats its
predicted quality as exact, so a user predicted just above a quality target may decode just below
it. The closest learned scheduler likewise assumes exact channel knowledge \cite{McDowell26}.
Moreover, the reported advantage of JSCC over separation-based transmission was measured against an
idealized digital link with exact channel knowledge and capacity-achieving codes
\cite{Bourtsoulatze19,Kurka20}. Whether this advantage persists under the reports of a real base
station remains open.
\end{itemize}
All three challenges raise a fundamental question, namely whether a learned code admits a link
abstraction. If such an abstraction exists, a further question is what makes a given code obey it.

To answer these questions, we trained a progressive code, which decodes from any prefix of its phases, under
slot-varying SNR, and tested candidate rules on held-out decodes with mixed slot SNRs
(Sections~\ref{sec:method-abstraction} and~\ref{sec:eval-abstraction}). We found three things.
First, its distortion adds up over slots: the source distortion plus a sensitivity-weighted sum of
per-slot minimum mean-square-error (MMSE) terms predicts held-out decodes with a mean absolute error
of 0.055--0.083~dB on two test sets, against 0.16--0.19~dB for EESM and 0.29--0.34~dB for MIESM with
the same per-phase weights. Linear analog codes obey this form by construction \cite{Jakubczak11};
here a nonlinear learned code acquires it. Second, training decides it: the same architecture
trained at one constant SNR per image obeys no such rule (the MMSE rule errs by 0.22~dB, and no rule
does better than 0.15~dB), because it codes its later phases against the base reconstruction it
expects at one SNR, the mismatch DeepJSCC-l observed for layers \cite{Kurka21}. Third, the
abstraction is cheap and accurate, yet its small error matters. Twelve decodes per image calibrate
it, and it predicts the decoded quality of complete scheduled deliveries with a mean absolute error
of 0.06--0.09~dB and a bias within $\pm 0.03$~dB, with exact, noisy or aged reports. At a hard
quality target, however, a scheduler that takes the calibrated curves as exact leaves an extra
1.7--2.0\% of the users below 30~dB, at every cell size from 16 to 128 users. In short, training
gives a learned code a link abstraction. With it, each user's state reduces to two numbers, the
phases received and their accumulated weighted noise, and scheduling becomes a planning problem,
provided the abstraction's residual error is part of the objective.

To address these challenges, we propose CLASP (Cal\-i\-bra\-tion-aware, Link-Ab\-strac\-tion-based Scheduling
of Progressive codes), a scheduler for learned progressive codes in a multi-user fading downlink.
Unlike existing JSCC methods, which value each user at one SNR or learn the allocation by
reinforcement learning, CLASP values every slot for every user through the code's link abstraction and plans each
user's delivery to the deadline exactly. It has one design for each challenge.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Link abstraction.} The progressive code is trained under slot-varying SNR and its MMSE
link abstraction is calibrated per image, so that each user becomes a two-number state.
\item \emph{Scheduler.} On this state the frame becomes a finite-horizon restless bandit
\cite{Whittle88}, a model already used to schedule layered video \cite{Hosseini19} and learned
semantic codes \cite{McDowell26}. A Lagrangian relaxation splits it into one small dynamic program per user under
per-slot prices, solved exactly on a CPU; the relaxation's value is an upper bound on every causal
scheduler; and users are served in the order of the Lagrangian index those programs define
\cite{Hao22,Brown20}.
\item \emph{Imperfect information.} Each program rewards the probability of reaching the quality
target under a calibrated predictive distribution of the user's decoded quality, rather than a hard
threshold on the predicted quality. The distribution's spread is the measured error of the
prediction, from the calibrated curves and from the channel reports. Given an aged report, each
program decides on the posterior of the channel, not on the report itself.
\end{itemize}

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Discovery.} We find that a learned progressive code trained under slot-varying SNR obeys
an additive MMSE link abstraction, more accurate than EESM and MIESM, while a code trained at
constant SNR obeys none, and that a transmitter can calibrate it from 12 decodes per image. To our
knowledge, effective-SNR mappings had not been tested on a learned code before.
\item \emph{Method.} We propose CLASP, which casts multi-user deadline scheduling of learned codes
as a finite-horizon restless bandit with a two-number user state, solves it with exact per-user
programs, a Lagrangian bound and an index policy, and is robust to the errors of its own quality
prediction and of the channel reports.
\item \emph{Performance.} Decoded by the codec, CLASP gains 0.5--0.6~dB of mean PSNR over PF and
brings an extra 8--15\% of the users to 30~dB over per-image rate selection, with fresh, noisy or
aged reports. In simulation its gap to the bound vanishes as the cell grows: from 0.21 to
0.005~dB per user between 8 and 128 users for mean PSNR, and for the target within $\pm 0.3\%$ of
the bound from 16 users on. Against a digital chain with CQI-table link adaptation and HARQ, it leads
at every CSI condition tested, by 1.2--1.6~dB and an extra 13--16\% of the users at 30~dB with
2~dB CQI errors or reports aged to correlation 0.8. An idealized digital code 3~dB from capacity
with the same HARQ leads CLASP by 2.4\% of the users with exact reports and trails it by 5--14\% from
1~dB CQI errors or correlation 0.95 on. Training the code on the channel CLASP creates adds
0.21~dB, decoded.
\end{itemize}
